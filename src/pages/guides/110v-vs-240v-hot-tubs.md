Every hot tub needs power for two big jobs: **heating the water** and **running the jet pumps**. How much power your spa can draw at once is set by its electrical service, either a standard **120-volt household outlet** (usually called "110V" or "plug-and-play") or a **dedicated 240-volt circuit** ("220V/240V"). That choice affects your installation cost, how fast the water heats, and how the spa behaves on a cold Utah night.

## The short answer

- **Choose 110V/120V plug-and-play** if you want the simplest, cheapest installation, have a smaller space (condo, townhome, patio), and mostly take shorter soaks.
- **Choose 240V** if you want a full-size spa, multiple pumps, long soaks in winter, or faster heat recovery, and you're willing to add a dedicated circuit.

## Why the voltage matters: the math

A standard 120V, 15-amp circuit can deliver at most 1,800 watts, and continuous loads like a spa heater are normally sized to 80% of that, about 1,440 watts. That's why plug-and-play spas use small heaters:

- **Garden Spas:** 1.0 kW heater at 120V (a 4.0 kW heater is listed for 240V hookups).
- **Nordic ALL-IN-110V:** 1 kW heater on 110V with a 1 hp, two-speed pump.

A 240V circuit can carry far more power, so full-size spas pair larger heaters with multiple pumps. For example, the [Artesian Elite](/spa-models/artesian-elite/) uses a 5.5 kW titanium heater.

Here's what those heater sizes mean for about 300 gallons of water, roughly the capacity of a 5–6 seat compact spa like the Garden Spas Plume (295 gallons). These figures are the **theoretical maximum before any heat loss**. In practice it's slower with the cover off or on a cold night.

| Heater | Approx. temperature rise per hour (300 gal) |
|---|---|
| 1 kW (typical 120V plug-and-play) | about 1.4 °F |
| 4 kW (typical 240V) | about 5.5 °F |
| 5.5 kW (Artesian Elite) | about 7.5 °F |

In plain terms, a 120V spa that cools off by 10 degrees can take most of a day to recover, while a 240V spa can do it in a couple of hours. That's the real difference you'll feel in a Utah winter.

## 110V / 120V plug-and-play hot tubs

**How they work:** the spa ships with a GFCI-protected cord and plugs into a standard outlet. Garden Spas specify a 120V/15A outlet and include a 15-foot GFCI cord. Nordic's ALL-IN-110V models come with the GFCI cord factory-installed.

**Pros**

- No new circuit or subpanel. That avoids the $800–$1,600 that HomeGuide lists for a typical new spa circuit.
- Flexible placement, including some indoor and condo or townhome locations (check your HOA).
- Faster to set up: level the base, fill it, plug it in.

**Trade-offs**

- Slow heat recovery, as shown above. Keep the cover on between soaks.
- Less total power for jets. Pumps on these models are typically single, two-speed units.
- Usually smaller, simpler spas.

**Models we carry:** [Garden Spas](/spa-models/garden-spas/) (2 to 6 seats), the [Nordic ALL-IN-110V Series](/spa-models/nordic-all-in-110v/) (Retreat, Stella, D'Amour, Warrior XL, Crown and Sport), and the South Seas 519P, a 4-seat model with a 120V plug-in cord and a 1.4 kW heater.

## 240V hot tubs

**How they work:** a licensed electrician runs a dedicated circuit from your panel to a GFCI-protected spa disconnect near the tub. Full-size spas typically call for a 40- to 60-amp circuit; Nordic, for example, lists 40A for its Sport Edition and 50A for the Encore LS. We'll confirm the exact requirement for your model before you buy.

**Pros**

- Much faster heating and recovery, which matters most from November through March.
- Room for bigger heaters, multiple jet pumps, and more features running at once.
- Full-size seating and stronger hydrotherapy.

**Trade-offs**

- Installation cost: a circuit, permit, and possibly a panel upgrade in older homes. HomeGuide lists $1,400–$2,800 for panel upgrades.
- The spa location is tied to where the wiring can reasonably run.

**Models we carry:** [Island Spas](/spa-models/island-spas/) and [Artesian Elite](/spa-models/artesian-elite/) (5.5 kW heaters), most [South Seas Spas](/spa-models/south-seas-spas/) (5.5 kW at 240V), the [Nordic Luxury](/spa-models/nordic-luxury/) and [Sport](/spa-models/nordic-sport/) series (220V), and [TidalFit swim spas](/spa-models/swim-spas/).

## The middle ground: convertible spas

Nordic lists its [Modern Series](/spa-models/nordic-modern/) and most [Classic Series](/spa-models/nordic-classic/) models as **110V/220V convertible**: a 1 kW heater on 110V or a 4 kW heater on 220V. That lets you start on a regular outlet and add a 240V circuit later if you want faster heating. Garden Spas also list a 4.0 kW heater option for 240V hookups. Ask us which specific models offer this.

## Side-by-side comparison

| | 110V / 120V plug-and-play | 240V |
|---|---|---|
| Electrical work | Standard outlet (dedicated circuit recommended) | New dedicated circuit and GFCI disconnect |
| Typical heater | about 1 kW | about 4–5.5 kW |
| Heat recovery | Slow (hours per few degrees) | Fast |
| Jets | Usually one two-speed pump | Often multiple pumps |
| Typical spa size | 2–6 seats, compact | 4–9 seats, full size |
| Best for | Condos, townhomes, small patios, budget installs | Families, long winter soaks, maximum hydrotherapy |

## Safety basics, whatever you choose

- Spa circuits must be GFCI-protected. Plug-and-play models include a GFCI cord, so use it as designed and don't use an extension cord.
- HomeGuide notes the spa disconnect should be at least 5 feet from the water. Your electrician will follow the current electrical code and pull any permit your city requires.
- Use a licensed electrician for any new circuit or panel work.

## How to decide

1. **How will you use it?** Short soaks a few times a week favor 120V. Long soaks, several people, or nightly winter use favor 240V.
2. **Where will it go?** If running a new circuit is hard or expensive at your home, plug-and-play may make the decision for you.
3. **What's your total budget?** Compare spa price + installation, not just the sticker. See our [Utah hot tub cost guide](/guides/hot-tub-cost-utah/).
